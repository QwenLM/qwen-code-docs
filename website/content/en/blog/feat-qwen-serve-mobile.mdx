You've handed Qwen Code a decent-sized job on your machine — refactoring a module and filling in the tests to match. It's reading files, editing code, running tests, doing just fine.

Then a meeting starts pulling at you, or you just want to leave the chair and collapse on the couch for a while.

Usually you have two options: sit and watch it finish before you move, or leave it running and check back later. Neither feels great.

But there's a third: pull out your phone and keep watching it work — approve when it needs approval, follow up when you want to — as if you never left the chair.

That's what `qwen serve` gives you.

## What it actually is

In one line: `qwen serve` turns the Qwen Code you're already using into a local service on your machine.

Your terminal, your IDE plugin, and any browser — including the one on your phone — all connect to the **same agent session**. The same conversation, the same file diffs, the same permission prompts.

It isn't a fresh cloud instance, and it doesn't sync your project anywhere else. It's the very same Qwen Code on your computer — it just opens one more door.

## One command opens the door

The best part is starting it — you don't even have to remember any flags.

Tell Qwen Code to "start remote access", or trigger the `serve` skill you set up, and it does the whole job for you. Under the hood, it roughly does this:

```bash
# 1. Find this machine's LAN IP
ipconfig getifaddr en0
# → 192.168.0.105

# 2. Start the service in the background (with a token, allowing your IP)
QWEN_SERVER_TOKEN=my-token qwen serve \
  --hostname 0.0.0.0 \
  --allow-origin "http://192.168.0.105:4170"
# → qwen serve listening on http://0.0.0.0:4170
#   (mode=http-bridge, workspace=/Users/you/your-project)
```

Then it hands you a **link with the token baked in, ready to click**:

```
http://192.168.0.105:4170?token=my-token
```

It handles three things you'd rather not do yourself: looking up the LAN IP, starting the service in the background, and stitching the token straight into the link. All you do is copy that one link.

That's what "easy" should feel like — the hassle hidden behind a single command, leaving you with just one action.

## On your phone, it's unbeatable

This is the highlight.

Connect your phone to the same Wi-Fi as your computer, paste that address into a browser, and you get a Web Shell you already know well — the same one as on your computer.

It's absolutely not a stripped-down chat box. Everything you see on your computer is here, none of it missing:

- Which file it read, which lines it changed — the diff, crystal clear
- Tool calls, collapsed; tap to see the details
- Before it touches your files or runs a command, it raises a permission card and waits for you to approve

You kicked it off on your computer; now you're lying on the couch watching it run. It's "Converting coffee into code..." on the screen over there, and you're watching it finish the job from over here.

Dropping the connection isn't a problem either. You step into an elevator, switch to 5G, switch back to Wi-Fi, and reopen the page — thanks to SSE reconnect, it fills in the part you missed. Not a blank page on refresh, but a seamless continuation from the exact line where you left.

## Why not something else?

You might think: couldn't I make do with something else for this?

**An IM bot?** (Telegram, DingTalk, Feishu, and the like)

Sure — but then every conversation, every chunk of code context, has to detour through a third party's IM server first. And what you see in the IM is a flattened plain-text bubble — no diff highlighting, no collapsible tool calls, no permission cards. `qwen serve` skips the middleman entirely: your browser connects straight to your own machine, showing a native, far denser interface.

**A cloud remote-control / cloud IDE?**

That means moving your whole development environment to the cloud: code, dependencies, secrets, your carefully configured git and shell. One move, and you're forever worrying the two sides don't match. `qwen serve` moves nothing — your environment stays exactly where it is, on your own machine; you just look at it through a different screen. Seamless switching during the workday, none of the "the cloud env isn't set up yet" mess.

When it comes down to it, these three things are what `qwen serve` actually wants to be:

**Restrained.** By default it listens only on `127.0.0.1`, sitting quietly on your machine; the outside world sees nothing.

**Secure.** The moment you open it to `0.0.0.0` for someone else (or your phone) to reach, it refuses to start without a token; CORS keeps every browser origin out by default; token checks use constant-time comparison, so even side channels are closed off.

**Private.** Your code, your secrets, your project — none of it ever leaves your machine. The root of trust is you, the user, yourself.

## A few details worth knowing

- **One session, many devices:** your computer and phone share the same session. Whichever device sees a permission request first can answer it — first-responder, no scrambling.
- **Voice input on your phone:** want to dictate instructions to it? Add `--tls-cert` / `--tls-key` to serve over HTTPS, and the browser's secure-context restriction lifts.
- **One daemon, many workspaces:** repeat `--workspace` and a single service can host several projects at once.

## An honest note on where it stands

Right now it's best suited to **local / LAN, single-user** scenarios, focused on text chat and coding. Heavier uses — multi-user collaboration, containerized deployment, public-internet exposure — are still being hardened in upcoming releases. In other words: running it on your own machine and continuing work from your phone is great today; using it as a production multi-tenant service — hold on a bit.

## Closing

A genuinely good tool is like this: strong in capability, but quiet by default; it shows up exactly when you need it, and only then.

`qwen serve` is exactly that. It didn't invent anything new — it just made "your agent should follow you around" the obvious thing it always should have been.

Next time you hand Qwen Code a long-running task, try standing up and pulling out your phone.

You'll find it's been there the whole time.
